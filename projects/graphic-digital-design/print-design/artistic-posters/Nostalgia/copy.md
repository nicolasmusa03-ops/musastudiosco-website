# Nostalgia

## Tagline
A visual letter to the person you used to be.

## Overview
This poster began as a personal exploration — no brief, no client, just a question: what does nostalgia actually look like when you refuse to make it pretty? The result is a piece about memory, growth, and the strange tenderness of looking back at who you were before you became who you are.

## Design Process
The image is the argument. A child running down a dirt road — carefree, mid-stride, moving toward something or away from something, it's impossible to tell which — is the most direct representation of the concept. But presenting that image cleanly felt dishonest. Memory doesn't work like that. It's blurred, obscured, partially reconstructed. Pixelating the child's face was the design decision that unlocked everything else. It creates presence without identity — you know someone is there, but you can't quite reach them. That's exactly how memory feels.

The typographic layer came from thinking about how repetition works in the mind. When a thought or feeling is significant enough, it loops. It replays. The quote — "May you become the person your past self needed" — repeats across the entire image field as a watermark, rendered in a bold white condensed sans that's dense enough to read as texture from a distance and as language up close. The closer you look, the more you understand. That layering of meaning at different viewing distances was intentional.

The film grain treatment was non-negotiable. Grain doesn't just add texture — it places the image in time. It signals that this was captured, that it existed, that the moment was real even if it now feels remote. The combination of the grain, the pixelation, and the typographic overlay builds three layers of distance between the viewer and the subject: visual noise, obscured identity, and the repetition of a wish rather than a fact.

The composition itself is understated — the child centered but not perfectly so, the horizon line low, the sky and road given room to breathe. There's no elaborate graphic geometry competing with the image. The design trusts the photograph.

## Design Principles Applied
- **Layered meaning** — the piece communicates differently at distance versus up close, rewarding attention
- **Deliberate degradation** — pixelation and grain are expressive choices, not limitations; they carry the emotional weight of imperfect memory
- **Typography as texture** — the repeated quote operates simultaneously as a design element and as the poster's emotional core
- **Restraint in composition** — no competing graphic elements; the image is given full authority
