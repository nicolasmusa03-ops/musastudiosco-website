# Pearl

## Tagline
Period horror, reframed as vintage glamour.

## Overview
Ti West's *Pearl* is a film about a woman trapped between the life she has and the life she imagines for herself — told through the visual language of 1950s Technicolor but with the soul of a slasher. Designing a poster for it meant honoring both halves of that identity: the lush, romantic surface and the violence beneath it.

## Design Process
The central compositional choice was the most important one: Mia Goth in full, standing center frame, in the red dress, holding the axe. The figure is quiet — her gaze downward, expression thoughtful rather than threatening — which makes the axe more disturbing, not less. I wanted the viewer to spend time with her before they noticed what she's holding.

The red dress in isolation is the only color in the piece. Everything else — the background, the panel photographs, the typography — runs in high-contrast black and white. This wasn't just stylistic; it was conceptual. Red is Pearl's color in the film: it's ambition, desire, and blood. Isolating it against monochrome forces the eye there and then won't let it go. It also solves the problem of making a horror poster feel glamorous — the dress reads as beautiful before it reads as ominous.

The panel layout on the right side of the composition borrows directly from classic Hollywood lobby card design — multiple rectangular film stills, stacked vertically, giving the viewer a secondary narrative. Rather than showing the most violent moments from the film, I selected images that show Pearl's range of emotion: the manic smile, the vulnerable gesture, the calculating stillness. The film's full horror comes from her interiority, not from the acts themselves.

Typography was handled with period awareness. The title treatment — italic quotation marks around "Pearl" in a high-contrast serif — references both old Hollywood poster typography and a slight self-awareness, as if even the film's marketing understands it's playing a role. The production credits and cast names at the bottom are set in a condensed serif strip that mirrors standard vintage poster billing blocks exactly.

The dark, heavily textured background — distressed and grain-treated — grounds everything in a sense of age without making the poster feel like a costume. The red X marks at the corners read as both decorative flourish and warning sign.

## Design Principles Applied
- **Selective color for impact** — a single color element in a monochrome composition commands disproportionate visual authority
- **Compositional restraint** — the central figure's quiet pose makes the weapon more unsettling than any action shot would
- **Historical typography fluency** — period-accurate type conventions make the poster feel native to the genre, not imitative
- **Panel layout as narrative** — secondary images are chosen for emotional range rather than spectacle
