# Science of Espresso

## Tagline
Dense information, made irresistible to look at.

## Overview
The challenge was self-imposed and specific: take genuinely complex, technical espresso science — roast levels, grind size, pressure, temperature, extraction time — and design a poster that makes all of it not just readable, but worth looking at. The goal wasn't simplification. It was seduction.

## Design Process
I turned to newspaper and editorial design because those traditions solved exactly this problem decades ago. The grid-based multi-column layout is a proven information architecture — it allows readers to scan before they commit, to drop in at any point, and to navigate dense content without feeling lost. Applying that structure to espresso science wasn't ironic; it was genuinely the right tool for the content.

The oversize "ESPRESSO" masthead dominates the top, set with aggressive kerning that lets the letters breathe into each other — a typographic choice that borrows from broadsheet newspaper logotypes where the publication's name has to establish authority instantly. "Behind The Extraction" sits beside it as a subheader, immediately framing the poster's intent: this is an investigation, not a pamphlet.

Within the columns, hierarchy is managed through a strict typographic system: bold category headers (ROAST LEVEL, GRIND SIZE, PRESSURE, etc.) function as section markers, followed by sub-category labels (Light, Medium, Dark) and body text at a reading size. The photography — a coffee grounds macro, a pressure gauge, a portafilter — is placed not decoratively but functionally, as visual proof of the concepts described in the text beside each image. They earn their space by illustrating something specific.

The monochrome palette was essential to the concept. Color would have made this feel like a branded infographic. Black, white, and gray make it feel like a scientific document — something produced because the information matters, not because someone paid for it. The aged, slightly textured paper quality reinforces that sense of authenticity.

What I wanted to prove with this piece is that information design can be a form of graphic design just as expressive as any purely visual work. The constraint of serving the content rigorously doesn't have to result in something sterile.

## Design Principles Applied
- **Editorial grid as creative structure** — newspaper column logic applied as a genuine solution to dense information, not as pastiche
- **Typography as navigation system** — consistent hierarchy levels (masthead → section header → sub-label → body) let readers find their entry point without instruction
- **Photography with purpose** — images are placed to illustrate adjacent text, not to fill space
- **Monochrome for credibility** — stripping color signals that the content is the point, not the presentation
