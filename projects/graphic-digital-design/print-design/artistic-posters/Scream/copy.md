# Scream

## Tagline
A horror poster that knows what it is.

## Overview
*Scream* is a film that deconstructs the horror genre from the inside — it knows the rules, explains them out loud, and then breaks them anyway. Designing a poster for it demanded the same self-awareness: a piece that operates fully within vintage horror poster conventions while making those conventions feel fresh through the specificity of its execution.

## Design Process
The aesthetic decision was exploitation cinema. Not a clean, modernized horror-adjacent design, but something that genuinely looks like it could have been printed on newsprint in 1996 and stapled to a telephone pole. Every element — the halftone photography, the distressed paper base, the blood-drip title treatment — exists to serve that reference without becoming parody.

"SCREAM" at the top is the anchor. Set in a massive condensed serif with visible bleed and grain, it's blood-red and treated as if the letters themselves are bleeding — not literally dripping, but with enough texture and roughness that the color reads as wet. Directly below it, the film's signature question — "What's your favorite scary movie?" — is set in a clean sans, creating a tonal whiplash between the visual violence above and the conversational register of the tagline. That's the film in two lines.

The main photograph — Skeet Ulrich, hand covering his face, expression ambiguous — is rendered in a halftone dot pattern that references both newspaper printing and the visual noise of a degraded VHS. This treatment collapses the image's photographic clarity into something grainier and more uncertain, which suits a film about not being able to trust what you're seeing. A red blood-smear overlaid on the hand is the only accent color in this section, placed so that the eye completes the threat the image only implies.

The three-panel strip below functions as both a horror film convention and a design solution. Three moments from the film — a phone call, a search, a glimpse of Ghostface — are presented in equal rectangular frames that read like film negatives. The bloody knife laid across the dividing line between the panels and the bottom section is the poster's most explicit visual pun: the knife that cuts through the film's genre pretensions, and the rule that separates two halves of the composition.

The billing block at the bottom is typeset with genuine attention to how vintage film credits were laid out — production company logos, cast names in condensed caps, the year sitting alone at the bottom right. Details that most people would never consciously notice, but that collectively make the piece feel earned rather than assembled.

## Design Principles Applied
- **Genre fluency as foundation** — deep familiarity with exploitation and vintage horror poster conventions makes subversion possible
- **Halftone as expressive medium** — the dot-screen treatment isn't a filter; it defines the entire tonal register of the piece
- **Tonal contrast in copy** — the tagline's conversational tone creates deliberate dissonance against the violent visual language
- **Detail as credibility** — accurate billing block formatting, period-correct type choices, and logo placement signal craft rather than costume
